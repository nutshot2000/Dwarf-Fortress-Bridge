# Telling the fort's story

The journal holds what really happened. A chapter turns it into something people want to hear: read aloud, narrated
over a video, or read like a book. `df_journal chapter` hands you the material; you write the prose;
`df_journal save_chapter` keeps it.

## The one rule

**Only what happened.** Every event, death, kill, quote and trait comes from the journal or the game. You may
choose, order, compress and comment, and you may say what a dwarf seems to feel when the game shows it (a thought,
a stress level, a breakdown). You may not invent events, dialogue or outcomes. When you don't know how something
ended, end the chapter on the question.

## The voice: funny by default

Dwarf Fortress is a dark comedy that takes itself completely seriously. Tell it the same way: a dry, deadpan
narrator who reports outrageous things in a calm voice, and lets the listener do the laughing. Unless the chapter
asks for another tone, this is the voice.

- **Understatement.** The flatter the delivery, the bigger the laugh. "Id lost his left eye to a badger. He has
  not discussed the badger since." A legendary axe lord brought down by a turkey is reported like any other death.
- **Timing.** Set it up, then land it in a short sentence of its own. Don't explain the joke, and don't stack
  three jokes in a paragraph: one good one is plenty.
- **The gap is the joke.** Dignity against circumstance: the mayor demanding a crown while the dining hall floods,
  the pacifist planter married to a weaponsmith, a fortress of ninety dwarves "joining the fight" against a buzzard
  circling a mile overhead.
- **Use the game's own precision.** DF describes injuries like a surgeon with no bedside manner ("the injured part
  explodes into gore"). Quoting it straight is funnier than any adjective. Gore is comedy when it's absurd and the
  dwarf survives it.
- **Running gags and callbacks.** The broker who still hasn't said a word. The dwarf who keeps taking baths and
  keeps not enjoying them. Bring them back; each return is funnier.
- **The overseer can be the punchline.** "I locked the door. I did not check who was still outside." A narrator
  who owns their blunders is good company.
- **Narrator's asides are allowed** (what the badger presumably thought of it all) as long as no listener could
  mistake them for something that happened.
- **Know when to stop.** A real loss (a child, a dwarf the story made us love, a fort-breaking disaster) gets
  played straight: no jokes in that paragraph, one sincere sentence, then the world goes on. Never mock grief.
  The jokes afterwards land better for it.

Other tones, when the chapter asks for them: **dramatic** (tension and loss, humour only in the margins) and
**epic** (a saga voice for sieges, megabeasts and last stands).

## Shape of a chapter

- **Open on a situation, not a cast list.** Something is happening or about to go wrong: a caravan nobody can
  greet, a door locked with someone behind it, a titan on the horizon. The first two sentences should make the
  listener want the third.
- **One thread, maybe two.** Pick the event that matters most since the last chapter and build around it. Routine
  (crops planted, bins made) is scenery: a sentence at most, or nothing.
- **Introduce people when they act.** A dwarf enters the story the first time they matter, with two or three
  details that fit the moment, never a list. The shy swordsman is introduced in the fight, not in a roll call.
  Characters already met get a name and a reminder ("Zuglar, still too shy to look anyone in the eye").
- **Two or three new characters per chapter at most.** The rest wait for their moment.
- **Stakes before outcomes.** Say what could be lost before saying what was lost. A death lands only if the
  listener met the dwarf first, so bring a dwarf in a chapter or two before their story peaks when you can.
- **End on a hook.** An open question, a threat arriving, a promise not yet kept. Never "and then things went on".
- **Length: 300 to 600 words.** One sitting, one listen.

## What makes it gripping

- **Contrast is the comedy and the tragedy.** The broker who never speaks. The pacifist planter married to a
  weaponsmith. The legendary weaponsmith whose dream is to master a skill. The warrior who dreams of starting a
  family. Look for the gap between who a dwarf is and what happens to them, and point at it once, lightly.
- **Small detail beats big summary.** "Kib was out collecting spider silk when the door was locked behind her"
  beats "a dwarf was trapped".
- **Let the dwarves speak.** The overheard lines are real; use the strange ones as they are, credited. One good
  quote per chapter is plenty.
- **The overseer is a character too.** The notes record what was decided and what went wrong; owning a mistake
  ("I locked the door. I did not check who was still outside.") is more gripping than any victory.
- **Callbacks.** Threads left open earlier (a lover far away, a dream, a grudge, a feud with the elves) pay off
  when they come back. Mention them when they do.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Short sentences for danger. Longer ones for quiet stretches and loss.

## Written to be heard

Chapters are often read aloud by a text-to-speech voice:

- No markdown inside the prose: no bullets, asterisks, tables or emoji. Paragraphs only, plus a one-line title.
- Numbers as words where a narrator would say them: "the year one hundred and two", "sixty-four".
- Spell out what symbols mean: not "☼alder bracelet☼" but "a masterwork alder bracelet".
- Keep names the way the game writes them; give a long name in full once, then use the first name.
- Refer to dwarves the way the game does (it gives each a sex); refer to the player as they.
